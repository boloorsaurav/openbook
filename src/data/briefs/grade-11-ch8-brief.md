### 2: Sequences
eyebrow: Section 8.2

**Concepts (list every distinct rule, definition, and pattern — be exhaustive):**
- The various numbers occurring in a sequence are called its terms.
- Terms of a sequence are denoted by a1, a2, a3, …, an, etc., where the subscripts denote the position of the term.
- The nth term is the number at the nth position of the sequence and is denoted by an.
- The nth term is also called the general term of the sequence.
- A sequence containing a finite number of terms is called a finite sequence.
- A sequence is called infinite if it is not a finite sequence.
- Often, it is possible to express the rule which yields the various terms of a sequence in terms of an algebraic formula.
- For example, the sequence of even natural numbers 2, 4, 6, … has nth term an = 2n, where n is a natural number.
- For the sequence of odd natural numbers 1, 3, 5, …, the nth term is an = 2n – 1, where n is a natural number.
- Some sequences have no visible pattern but are generated by a recurrence relation, such as the Fibonacci sequence: a1 = a2 = 1, and an = an – 2 + an – 1 for n > 2.
- Some sequences, like the sequence of primes 2, 3, 5, 7, …, have no formula for the nth prime and can only be described by verbal description.
- In every sequence, we expect a theoretical scheme or a rule for generating the terms a1, a2, a3, …, an, … in succession.
- A sequence can be regarded as a function whose domain is the set of natural numbers or some subset of it.
- Sometimes, the functional notation a(n) is used for an.

**Worked examples:**
- Example 1: Finding the first three terms of sequences defined by an = 2n + 5 and an = (n – 3)/4, demonstrating substitution of n = 1, 2, 3.
- Example 2: Finding the 20th term of the sequence defined by an = (n – 1)(2 – n)(3 + n), demonstrating substitution of n = 20.
- Example 3: Finding the first five terms and corresponding series for a sequence defined by a1 = 1, an = an – 1 + 2 for n ≥ 2, demonstrating a recurrence relation.

### 3: Series
eyebrow: Section 8.3

**Concepts (list every distinct rule, definition, and pattern — be exhaustive):**
- Let a1, a2, a3, …, an, … be a given sequence. Then the expression a1 + a2 + a3 + … + an + … is called the series associated with the given sequence.
- The series is finite or infinite according as the given sequence is finite or infinite.
- Series are often represented in compact form, called sigma notation, using the Greek letter Σ (sigma) as a means of indicating the summation involved.
- The series a1 + a2 + a3 + ... + an is abbreviated as Σ ak from k=1 to n.
- When the term "series" is used, it refers to the indicated sum, not to the sum itself.
- For example, 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 is a finite series with four terms.
- When we use the phrase "sum of a series," we mean the number that results from adding the terms. The sum of the series 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 is 16.

**Worked examples:**
- None.

### 4: Geometric Progression (G.P.)
eyebrow: Section 8.4

**Concepts (list every distinct rule, definition, and pattern — be exhaustive):**
- A sequence a1, a2, a3, …, an, … is called a geometric progression if each term is non-zero and the ratio of any term to its preceding term is constant.
- This constant ratio is called the common ratio, denoted by r.
- For a G.P. with first term a and common ratio r, the terms are: a, ar, ar squared, ar cubed, …
- The nth term (general term) of a G.P. is given by an = a times r raised to the power (n – 1).
- A finite G.P. can be written as a, ar, ar squared, …, a times r raised to the power (n – 1).
- An infinite G.P. can be written as a, ar, ar squared, …, a times r raised to the power (n – 1), …
- The series a + ar + ar squared + ... + a times r raised to the power (n – 1) is called a finite geometric series.
- The series a + ar + ar squared + ... + a times r raised to the power (n – 1) + ... is called an infinite geometric series.
- The sum to n terms of a G.P., denoted Sn, is given by:
    - If r = 1, Sn = n times a.
    - If r ≠ 1, Sn = a times (1 – r raised to the power n) divided by (1 – r), or equivalently, a times (r raised to the power n – 1) divided by (r – 1).
- The geometric mean (G.M.) of two positive numbers a and b is the square root of (a times b).
- If a, G, b are in G.P., then G is the geometric mean of a and b.
- Given any two positive numbers a and b, we can insert n numbers G1, G2, …, Gn between them such that the resulting sequence a, G1, G2, …, Gn, b is a G.P.
- For n geometric means between a and b, the common ratio r is given by r = (b/a) raised to the power 1/(n+1).
- The geometric means are then: G1 = a times r, G2 = a times r squared, …, Gn = a times r raised to the power n.

**Worked examples:**
- Example 4: Finding the 10th and nth terms of the G.P. 5, 25, 125, …, demonstrating the use of the formula an = a times r raised to the power (n – 1).
- Example 5: Finding which term of the G.P. 2, 8, 32, … is 131072, demonstrating solving for n using the nth term formula.
- Example 6: Finding the 10th term of a G.P. given the 3rd term is 24 and the 6th term is 192, demonstrating solving for a and r using a system of equations.
- Example 7: Finding the sum of the first n terms and the sum of the first 5 terms of the geometric series 1 + 2/3 + 4/9 + …, demonstrating the sum formula for r ≠ 1.
- Example 8: Finding how many terms of the G.P. 3, 3/2, 3/4, … are needed to give the sum 3069/512, demonstrating solving for n using the sum formula.
- Example 9: Finding the common ratio and terms of a G.P. where the sum of the first three terms is 13/12 and their product is –1, demonstrating solving a system of equations with terms a/r, a, ar.
- Example 10: Finding the sum of the sequence 7, 77, 777, 7777, … to n terms, demonstrating relating a non-G.P. sequence to a G.P. by rewriting terms.
- Example 11: Finding the number of ancestors a person has in the ten generations preceding his own, given 2 parents, 4 grandparents, 8 great grandparents, etc., demonstrating a real-world application of the sum of a G.P.
- Example 12: Inserting three numbers between 1 and 256 so that the resulting sequence is a G.P., demonstrating the concept of geometric means.

**Callout cards:**
- title: "Geometric Progression (G.P.)" | content: "A sequence where each term (except the first) is obtained by multiplying the previous term by a constant called the common ratio (r)."

### 5: Relationship Between A.M. and G.M.
eyebrow: Section 8.5

**Concepts (list every distinct rule, definition, and pattern — be exhaustive):**
- Let A and G be the arithmetic mean (A.M.) and geometric mean (G.M.) of two given positive real numbers a and b, respectively.
- A = (a + b)/2 and G = square root of (a times b).
- The relationship between A and G is: A – G = (a + b)/2 – square root of (a times b) = (a + b – 2 times square root of (a times b))/2 = (square of (square root of a – square root of b))/2, which is greater than or equal to 0.
- Therefore, A is greater than or equal to G (A ≥ G). The equality holds when a = b.

**Worked examples:**
- Example 13: Finding two positive numbers a and b given that their A.M. is 10 and G.M. is 8, demonstrating the use of the relationship between A.M. and G.M. to solve for a and b.

**Callout cards:**
- title: "A.M. ≥ G.M." | content: "For any two positive real numbers a and b, the arithmetic mean (A) is always greater than or equal to the geometric mean (G). They are equal only when a = b."

**Widget idea:** An interactive tool where users can input two positive numbers a and b, and the tool calculates and displays their A.M., G.M., and the difference A – G, visually demonstrating the inequality.

**Key terms:** arithmetic mean, geometric mean, A.M., G.M., inequality
