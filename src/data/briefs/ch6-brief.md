### 1: Some Properties of Multiplication
eyebrow: Distributive Property and Product Changes

**Concepts (exhaustive — every rule, definition, pattern):**
- The distributive property of multiplication over addition states a(b + c) = ab + ac.
- The distributive property can be visualised using a rectangular area diagram.
- Using commutativity, (a + b)c = ac + bc.
- If one number in a product ab is increased by 1, the product increases by a: a(b + 1) = ab + a.
- If both numbers in a product ab are increased by 1, the product increases by a + b + 1: (a + 1)(b + 1) = ab + a + b + 1.
- If a is increased by 1 and b is decreased by 1, the product changes by b - a - 1: (a + 1)(b - 1) = ab + b - a - 1.
- The distributive property holds for integers as well.
- Two algebraic expressions are equal if they take the same values when their letter-numbers are replaced by numbers.
- An identity is a mathematical statement expressing the equality of two algebraic expressions.
- The general identity for increasing a by m and b by n is (a + m)(b + n) = ab + an + bm + mn.
- The product (a + m)(b + n) is the sum of the product of each term of (a + m) with each term of (b + n).
- The identity (a + m)(b + n) = ab + an + bm + mn can be visualised as a rectangle divided into four smaller rectangles.
- Subtraction can be handled by treating it as addition of a negative number in the general identity.
- The product (a + u)(b - v) expands to ab + ub - av - uv.
- The product (a - u)(b + v) expands to ab - ub + av - uv.
- The product (a - u)(b - v) expands to ab - ub - av + uv.
- The distributive property is not restricted to two terms within a bracket.
- Like terms have the same letter-numbers and can be combined into a single term.
- Brahmagupta first explicitly stated the distributive property as khaṇḍa-guṇanam (multiplication by parts).
- The distributive property can be used for fast multiplication, e.g., multiplying by 11, 101, 1001.
- Multiplying a number by 11 is equivalent to adding the number to itself shifted by one decimal place.
- Multiplying a number by 101 is equivalent to adding the number to itself shifted by two decimal places.

**Worked examples (1 sentence each, only if source walks through solution):**
- Example 1: Expand (3a/2)(a - b + 5) using the distributive property, resulting in (3/2)a² - (3/2)ab + (3/10)a.
- Example 2: Expand (a + b)(a + b) using the distributive property, resulting in a² + 2ab + b².
- Example 3: Expand (a + b)(a² + 2ab + b²) by distributing and combining like terms, resulting in a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³.
- Worked example: Multiply 3874 × 11 by writing 11 as (10 + 1) and adding 38740 + 3874.
- Worked example: Multiply 3874 × 101 by writing 101 as (100 + 1) and adding 387400 + 3874.
- Worked example: Multiply a 4-digit number dcba by 11, showing the pattern of adding adjacent digits.

- title: "Identity" | content: "An identity is a mathematical statement that expresses the equality of two algebraic expressions and holds true for all values of the variables."
- title: "General Product Identity" | content: "(a + m)(b + n) = ab + an + bm + mn. The product is the sum of the product of each term of the first expression with each term of the second."

### 2: Special Cases of the Distributive Property
eyebrow: Squares and Differences

**Concepts (exhaustive — every rule, definition, pattern):**
- The square of the sum of two numbers is given by Identity 1A: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b².
- Identity 1A can be visualised as the area of a square of side (a + b) divided into four regions.
- The square of the difference of two numbers is given by Identity 1B: (a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b².
- Identity 1B can be visualised by subtracting areas from a larger square and adding back the doubly-subtracted area.
- Identity 1B can also be derived from Identity 1A by writing (a - b)² as (a + (-b))².
- The product of the sum and difference of two numbers is given by Identity 1C: (a + b)(a - b) = a² - b².
- Identity 1C can be visualised geometrically by rearranging areas.
- Identity 1C can be rearranged to compute squares: a² = (a + b)(a - b) + b².
- Pattern 1: 2(a² + b²) = (a + b)² + (a - b)².
- Pattern 2: a² - b² = (a + b)(a - b).

**Worked examples (1 sentence each, only if source walks through solution):**
- Worked example: Find 65² by splitting 65 into 60 + 5 and using (60 + 5)² = 60² + 5² + 2(60 × 5) = 4225.
- Worked example: Find 55² by splitting 55 into 60 - 5 and using (60 - 5)² = 60² - 2(60 × 5) + 5² = 3025.
- Worked example: Expand (6x + 5)² using both the distributive property and Identity 1A, both resulting in 36x² + 60x + 25.
- Worked example: Find 31² using a² = (a + b)(a - b) + b² with a = 31 and b = 1, resulting in 32 × 30 + 1 = 961.
- Worked example: Find 197² using a² = (a + b)(a - b) + b² with a = 197 and b = 3, resulting in 200 × 194 + 9 = 38809.

**Callout cards (1-3 per section):**
- title: "Square of a Sum" | content: "(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b². The square of a sum is the sum of the squares plus twice the product."
- title: "Square of a Difference" | content: "(a - b)² = a² - 2ab + b². The square of a difference is the sum of the squares minus twice the product."
- title: "Difference of Squares" | content: "(a + b)(a - b) = a² - b². The product of the sum and difference of two numbers equals the difference of their squares."

**Widget idea:** An interactive square area model where users can adjust a and b to see the geometric decomposition for (a+b)² and (a-b)².

**Key terms:** square of a sum, square of a difference, difference of squares

### 4: This Way or That Way, All Ways Lead to the Bay
eyebrow: Multiple Methods for Patterns

**Concepts (exhaustive — every rule, definition, pattern):**
- A single pattern can be described by multiple different algebraic expressions.
- Different expressions describing the same pattern are equivalent and simplify to the same form.
- Finding multiple ways to solve a problem is a creative process in mathematics.
- The number of circles in a triangular pattern at Step k can be expressed as (k+1)² - 1, k² + 2k, k(k+1) + k, or k(k+2), all of which simplify to k² + 2k.
- The area of a shaded region can be found using multiple methods, such as subtracting areas or finding the area of a constituent shape.
- Different methods for finding an area will yield equivalent algebraic expressions.

**Worked examples (1 sentence each, only if source walks through solution):**
- Worked example: Find the number of circles in Step k of a pattern using four different methods, all simplifying to k² + 2k.
- Worked example: Find the area of an interior shaded region using Tadang's method [(m+n)² - 4mn] and Yusuf's method [(n-m)²], and verify they are equal.
- Worked example: Find the area of a region with slanting lines using Anusha's method (x² - xy), Vaishnavi's method [x(x+2y) - 3xy], and Aditya's method [x(x-y)], and verify they are equivalent.
