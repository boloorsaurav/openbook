### 1: Reema’s Curiosity and the Mechanism of Counting
eyebrow: The Need for Numbers

**Concepts (exhaustive — every rule, definition, pattern):**
- The modern oral and written number system (Hindu numerals) originated in India thousands of years ago, with names for powers of 10 (eka, dasha, shata, etc.) appearing in ancient texts like the Yajurveda Samhita.
- The digits 0 through 9 were developed in India around 2000 years ago; the first known instance of numbers written using ten digits (including 0 as a dot) is in the Bakhshali manuscript (c. 3rd century CE).
- Aryabhata (c. 499 CE) was the first mathematician to fully explain and do elaborate scientific computations with the Indian system of 10 symbols.
- The Indian number system was transmitted to the Arab world by around 800 CE, popularised by Al-Khwārizmī and Al-Kindi, then transmitted to Europe and Africa by around 1100 CE, and adopted widely by the 17th century.
- The terms "Arabic numerals" and "Hindu numerals" reflect historical perspectives; the system is now called Hindu numerals, Indian numerals, or Hindu-Arabic numerals.
- To count without number names, one can use a one-to-one mapping between objects and a standard sequence (e.g., sticks, sounds of letters, or written symbols).
- A number system is a standard sequence of objects, names, or written symbols that has a fixed order, used for counting by making a one-to-one mapping.
- Using sticks gives an unending standard sequence but is inconvenient for large collections; using sounds of letters is convenient but not unending; the Roman system (Method 3) was used in Europe but cannot write arbitrarily large numbers without introducing more symbols.
- Numerals are the symbols occurring in a written number system (e.g., 0, 1, 5, 36, 193 in the Hindu system).

### 2: Some Early Number Systems
eyebrow: Body Parts, Tally Marks, and Counting in Groups

**Concepts (exhaustive — every rule, definition, pattern):**
- Many groups of people used body parts (e.g., hands) as a standard sequence for counting.
- Tally marks are notches cut on a surface (bone, wall) to represent numbers, similar to using sticks; one mark is made for each object.
- The Ishango bone (20,000-35,000 years old) and Lebombo bone (c. 44,000 years old) are the oldest known tally sticks, possibly used as calendars.
- The Gumulgal people of Australia formed number names by counting in twos: 3 = 2+1, 4 = 2+2, 5 = 2+2+1, 6 = 2+2+2; any number greater than 6 was called "ras".
- The Bakairi people of South America and the Bushmen of South Africa independently developed equivalent number systems (counting in twos), suggesting a common ancestral origin.
- Counting in groups of a certain size (like 2, 5, 10, or 20) is more efficient than tally systems; this idea is an important breakthrough in the evolution of number systems.
- The Roman numeral system uses landmark numbers: I (1), V (5), X (10), L (50), C (100), D (500), M (1000).
- To write a number in Roman numerals up to 39, group it into as many 10s as possible, then 5s, then 1s (e.g., 27 = 10+10+5+1+1 = XXVII).
- For numbers like 4 and 40, subtractive notation is used (IV = 4, XL = 40), though this was not always consistent.
- To write larger numbers in Roman numerals (e.g., 2367), sum the landmark numbers starting from the largest (M, D, C, L, X, V, I) taking as many of each as possible.
- The Roman system is efficient for representation but does not lend itself to easy arithmetic (addition, multiplication, division) without tools like the abacus.

**Worked examples (1 sentence each, only if source walks through solution):**
- Example: 27 in Roman numerals is XXVII (10+10+5+1+1).
- Example: 2367 in Roman numerals is MMCCCLXII (1000+1000+100+100+100+50+10+5+1+1).
- Example: Adding CCXXXII + CCCCXIII in Roman numerals involves counting totals of I, X, and C, then grouping 5 Cs into a D (500) to get the sum.

- title: "Counting in Groups" | content: "Counting in groups of a certain size (like 2, 5, 10, or 20) is more efficient than tally systems. This idea, seen in the Gumulgal and Roman systems, is a key step in the evolution of number systems."

**Widget idea:** An interactive Roman numeral converter where students type a number and see its Roman numeral representation, or vice versa.

### 3: The Idea of a Base
eyebrow: Egyptian System and Base-n Systems

**Concepts (exhaustive — every rule, definition, pattern):**
- The Egyptian number system (c. 3000 BCE) uses landmark numbers that are powers of 10: 1, 10, 100, 1000, etc., each with a unique symbol.
- To represent a number in the Egyptian system, count it in groups of the landmark numbers, starting from the largest landmark number less than the given number.
- A base-n number system has landmark numbers where the first is 1, and every next landmark number is obtained by multiplying the current landmark number by a fixed number n.
- The Egyptian system is a base-10 (decimal) system; a system where each landmark number is 5 times the previous is a base-5 system.
- The landmark numbers of a base-n system are the powers of n: n^0 = 1, n, n^2, n^3, ...
- The advantage of a base-n system is that the product of any two landmark numbers is another landmark number, simplifying multiplication.
- Multiplying a number by a landmark number in the Egyptian system (e.g., by 10) increases the power of 10 by 1, giving the next landmark number.
- The distributive property holds for multiplication in base-n systems, allowing multiplication of multi-term numbers.
- The abacus (c. 11th century) used a decimal system with lines for powers of 10 and counters; a counter above a line contributed a value of 5.
- The Egyptian system has a drawback: representing very large numbers requires inventing an unending sequence of new symbols for higher powers of 10.

**Worked examples (1 sentence each, only if source walks through solution):**
- Example: 324 in the Egyptian system is represented as three 100 symbols, two 10 symbols, and four 1 symbols.
- Example: 143 in a base-5 system is 125 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1.
- Example: Adding two Egyptian numerals involves counting totals of each symbol and regrouping 10 of a smaller landmark into one of the next larger landmark.
- Example: Multiplying a number by 10 in the Egyptian system increases the power of 10 by 1 (e.g., a landmark number times 10 gives the next landmark number).
- Example: Multiplying a multi-term number by a landmark number uses the distributive property (e.g., (a+b+c) × n = an + bn + cn).

**Callout cards (1-3 per section):**
- title: "Base-n Number System" | content: "A number system where the first landmark number is 1, and every next landmark number is obtained by multiplying the current one by a fixed number n. The landmark numbers are the powers of n: n^0, n^1, n^2, n^3, ..."
- title: "Advantage of a Base" | content: "In a base-n system, the product of any two landmark numbers is another landmark number. This simplifies multiplication, unlike in the Roman system where landmark numbers do not follow a consistent multiplicative pattern."

**Widget idea:** An interactive abacus simulation where students add numbers by moving counters on lines representing powers of 10.

**Key terms:** Egyptian number system, base-n number system, base-10, decimal system, base-5, landmark numbers, powers of 10, distributive property, abacus

### 4: Place Value Representation
eyebrow: Mesopotamian, Mayan, Chinese, and Hindu Systems

**Concepts (exhaustive — every rule, definition, pattern):**
- The Mesopotamian (Babylonian) number system became a base-60 (sexagesimal) system, with symbols for 1 and 10 used to represent numbers from 1 to 59.
- To represent a number in the Mesopotamian system, group it into powers of 60 (60^0, 60^1, 60^2, ...), and no power of 60 can occur 60 or more times.
- The Mesopotamians made the representation more compact by dropping symbols for the powers of 60 and using position: the rightmost set of symbols shows the number of 1s, the next left shows the number of 60s, the next shows the number of 3600s, and so on.
- A positional number system (or place value system) uses the position of each symbol to determine the landmark number it is associated with.
- The Mesopotamian system had defects: blank spaces for missing powers of 60 led to ambiguity, and later a placeholder symbol (like zero) was used to denote blank spaces, but it was not used at the end of numbers.
- The Mayan civilisation developed an independent place value system with a base of 20 (but with a third landmark of 360 instead of 400, possibly due to calendars), using a dot for 1, a bar for 5, and a seashell-like symbol for zero.
- The Chinese rod numerals (c. 3rd century AD) were a decimal (base-10) place value system using two sets of symbols (zong and heng) that alternated by place value to avoid ambiguity, with a blank space for zero.
- The Hindu number system is a base-10 place value system using ten symbols (0-9), with a symbol for zero as early as 200 BCE.
- Because of the use of 0 as a digit in each position, the Hindu system allows unambiguous reading and writing of all numbers using only finitely many symbols.
- Zero was given the status of a number in its own right in Indian mathematics; Aryabhata (499 CE) used its arithmetic properties, and Brahmagupta (628 CE) codified operations with zero and negative numbers, creating what is now called a ring.
- The Hindu number system is now used throughout the world and is considered one of the greatest inventions in human history.

**Worked examples (1 sentence each, only if source walks through solution):**
- Example: 640 in the Mesopotamian system is (10) × 60 + 40, represented compactly by the symbols for 10 and 40 in the appropriate positions.
- Example: 7530 in the Mesopotamian system is (2) × 3600 + (5) × 60 + 30.
- Example: 2634 in the Chinese rod numeral system uses alternating zong and heng symbols for each place value (2 heng, 6 zong, 3 heng, 4 zong).
- Example: 375 in the Hindu system is read as (3) × 10^2 + (7) × 10 + (5) × 1.

**Callout cards (1-3 per section):**
- title: "Place Value System" | content: "A positional number system (or place value system) uses the position of each symbol to determine the landmark number it is associated with. This removes the need for an unending sequence of symbols for landmark numbers."
- title: "The Role of Zero" | content: "Zero is indispensable as a placeholder in a place value system to avoid ambiguity. The Hindu number system uses 0 as a digit in each position, allowing unambiguous representation of all numbers using only finitely many symbols."
- title: "Evolution of Ideas in Number Representation" | content: "1. Count in groups of a single number. 2. Group using landmark numbers. 3. Choose powers of a number as landmark numbers (base). 4. Use positions to denote landmark numbers (place value). 5. Use 0 as a positional digit and as a number."

**Widget idea:** An interactive place value chart where students drag and drop symbols (Mesopotamian, Mayan, Chinese, or Hindu) into the correct positions to represent a given number.

**Key terms:** Mesopotamian number system, sexagesimal system, base-60, positional number system, place value system, placeholder, zero, Mayan number system, Chinese rod numerals, zong, heng, Hindu number system, decimal system, Brahmagupta, ring
